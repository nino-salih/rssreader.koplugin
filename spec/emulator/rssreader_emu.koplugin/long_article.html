The small library at the end of Harbour Street opened its doors again on Monday, after eleven months of repairs that cost more than anyone had planned. Readers queued in the rain before nine o'clock. Some of them had brought back books they borrowed before the flood.

"We stopped counting the late fees," said the head librarian, Dr. Ruth Calder, who has run the branch since 2009. "People returned what they could. That was enough for us."

What the water took

The flood came at night, when the river rose faster than the forecast had said. By the morning the ground floor stood half a metre under water. The children's section was gone, and so were the local newspapers from the years 1921 to 1958, which had never been copied. The council sent pumps on the second day. A team of volunteers carried the wet books into a school gym, where they dried on long tables for weeks. Most of them could not be saved. The paper swelled, the glue let go and the pages turned grey with mould. "You could smell it from the car park," one of the volunteers remembered. Still, about four hundred volumes came through, e.g. a set of parish registers and a box of maps that a farmer had given to the library in the 1960s. They are now kept in a dry room on the upper floor, behind a door with a small window. Visitors can look at them by appointment. The library wants to scan the maps first, because farmers and builders still ask for them when they argue about old field borders. Nobody knows yet how long the scanning will take… The money for it has not been found. The council has promised to look at the question again in spring.

The building itself was in worse shape than it looked. The old wooden floor had to come out completely, and below it the builders found the remains of an earlier floor, laid in stone, with a drain that nobody had known about. The architects changed their plans twice. The new floor sits on a raised frame, so that water can pass under it in the next flood without touching the shelves.

A different kind of reading room

More tables, fewer shelves

The new ground floor has fewer shelves and more tables. Ms. Calder says this was the most difficult decision of the whole project. Many readers wanted every book back where it had been; others asked for space to work, to meet and to sit with a newspaper for an hour without buying a coffee. The library chose the second group, at least for the ground floor. The books did not disappear. They moved upstairs, where a new lift now makes them easier to reach for older readers and for parents with prams. On the ground floor, long oak tables stand under the windows, each with a lamp and a socket. There is a corner for the children with low benches and a carpet that can be lifted and dried. The local history collection has its own room at the back, with a glass wall, so that people walking past can see what the library keeps. "It is a reading room again," Dr. Calder said. "That is what it was built for in 1887, and that is what the town needs now." The opening hours are longer than before the flood: the library is open on six days a week, and on Thursdays until eight in the evening. On Saturdays volunteers help visitors who want to read the old newspapers on the new screens. More than sixty people have already signed up for the first course, which teaches how to search the scanned pages by name, street or date. The course is free. People who cannot come to the library can follow it online, and the recordings stay on the library's website for a year. Some readers still miss the old room, with its dark shelves and the smell of floor polish. Most of them, however, seem to like the light. On the first morning, every table was taken by half past ten!

The library also changed the way it lends books. Readers can now borrow up to twenty items at once, and they can keep them for six weeks instead of three. A new machine at the entrance takes returns at any time of the day. Late fees are gone for good. The council decided that the fees cost more to collect than they brought in, and that they kept poorer families away from the library.

  • Monday to Wednesday: 9 to 18 o'clock.
  • Thursday: 9 to 20 o'clock, with the newspaper course from 18 o'clock.
  • Friday and Saturday: 9 to 16 o'clock. Sunday and public holidays: closed.

Who paid for it

The repairs cost 2.4 million pounds. The insurance paid a little more than half. The rest came from the council, from a national fund for flood damage and from a public collection that raised 186,000 pounds in four months, which is more than the town had ever collected for anything before. More than three thousand people gave money, many of them small amounts. A local baker sold a "library loaf" for a year and gave the profit to the fund. The school children ran, swam and baked for it. The names of all donors who agreed to be named are written on a long board in the entrance hall, and the list is also on the library's website.

"A town that keeps its library open in a flood year has decided what kind of town it wants to be," the mayor said at the opening. "We should not forget that, when the next budget comes."

Not everybody agrees; some councillors would have preferred to spend the money on the flood walls along the river, and they said so in the council meeting in March; the vote was close, with eleven votes for the library and nine against, and the debate is not over, since the river is still there and the next winter will come

The library plans a small exhibition about the flood for the autumn. It will show photographs, the dried maps and some of the books that could not be saved, as a warning and as a memory. Anyone who has pictures from those days is asked to bring them to the front desk. The staff will scan them and return them the same day.

For now, the readers are simply glad to be back. An old man who comes every morning at nine to read the newspapers put it in a few words: "It is warm, it is dry, and nobody asks me to buy anything. Where else can you find that?"
